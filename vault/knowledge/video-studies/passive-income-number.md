## The hook — one hook, two operators, near-verbatim

This is the single strongest finding in the study, because it is **the thing two
unrelated teams did the same way**.

**A (0–≈8%, ≈0:00–0:38):**
> *"Imagine waking up on a Wednesday morning in Singapore. You make yourself a
> co[ffee]. You sit by the window and you check your phone, not because you have to,
> but because you want to see how much your investments paid you while you were asleep."*

**B (0–≈8%, ≈0:00–0:55):**
> *"You wake up at 6:14 in the morning. You did not set an alarm. You walk to the
> kitchen. You make coffee. You sit at the window. Your phone buzzes once. $41 paid to
> you while you were sleeping. Not from your job. Not from a side hustle. From owning."*

**Hook type: second-person future-state simulation.** Not a pain-mirror (nobody's bad
month is narrated), not a stat, not a self-introduction, not number-first. Both put the
viewer inside the *won* version of his morning and let the money arrive without him.
The shared beat sequence is: **wake → no alarm → coffee → window → phone → money that
arrived while you slept.** Six beats, same order, two channels.

Two deltas inside the same hook, and B's is the better one:

- **A announces the promise (≈5%, ≈0:22):** *"Today, I'm going to show you exactly how
  much you need invested to live off dividends forever. The numbers are more achievable
  than you think."* Then it immediately re-closes the loop to buy runway: *"Before the
  numbers, there is one thing about Singapore that most investors completely overlook."*
  Promise made, then deliberately deferred by ~14% of runtime.
- **B never announces — it names the withheld noun (≈7%, ≈0:45):** *"You did not get
  rich. You just hit a number. A specific number. One that pays your rent, your
  groceries, your gas, the life you used to fund with your hours."* The number is the
  title's promise and B refuses to say it. The entire video is the answer.

**Both promise inside the first ~8% of runtime** (A ≈0:22, B ≈0:45). A's is an
announcement; B's is an open loop. Given our `no_return_promise` constraint, B's form is
also the safer one: an open loop commits to nothing, an announcement of "how much you
need … forever" commits to permanence.

Both close the loop they opened, on the same image:
A — *"Just like a paycheck except no commute, no boss, and no alarm clock you have to
obey."* B (final line) — *"Now you own your mornings forever."*

## The assumption sentences — verbatim (deliverable #2)

This is the beat `run.json.constraints.withdrawal_rate_on_screen` and `no_return_promise`
exist to govern. Both twins state a rate; only one of them keeps stating it.

**A — states the assumption once, explicitly, as a choice, and puts the divisor inside
the arithmetic sentence:**
> *"A well-built Singapore dividend portfolio blending blue chips and REITs realistically
> yields between 4.5% and 6%. **We will use 5% as our conservative working number.**"*
> → *"To generate $30,000 a year, **you divide by 5%** and need $600,000 invested."*

The phrase *"we will use … as our conservative working number"* is the single most
directly reusable sentence in this study. It marks the rate as an **assumption the
narrator chose**, not a rate the market owes you — and the operative divisor is spoken
in the same breath as the corpus, which is exactly what our on-screen rule demands.

**B — states the rate once at rung 1, then never again for six more numbers:**
> *"At a **4% yield**, that 10,000 pays you $400 a year."*

Every later rung is silently the same 4% (50k→2k, 100k→4k, 250k→10k, 500k→20k, 1M→40k,
1.5M→60k — arithmetically exact throughout), but the rate is **not re-spoken with the
number**. It resurfaces only at rung 5, and when it does it is *mislabelled*:
> *"This is the **famous 4% rule**. The number financial planners have used for 30 years."*

**That conflates a 4% dividend yield with the 4% safe-withdrawal rule.** They are
different objects: one is income thrown off by the portfolio, the other is a drawdown
rate that may consume principal. B then compounds it — *"You do not touch the principle
[principal]. The principle keeps growing"* — an implied capital-preservation promise
that the 4% rule does not make.

## Transferable to a ₹ cut with "dividend" banned? (deliverable #4)

**Verdict: B transfers almost intact. A transfers only as a shape. Build the ₹ cut on
B's spine.** Argued from the transcripts:

1. **B's mechanism never needs the word.** Its actual engine is `corpus × rate = annual
   income, expressed as named bills`. Nothing in that sentence is dividend-specific.
   Swap the instrument to a SIP-accumulated index/hybrid corpus and an **SWP** payout and
   the ladder is unchanged: ₹X invested → ₹Y per month → *that* is your bijli bill, your
   ration, your petrol, your EMI.
2. **SWP is a *better* fit than dividends for this exact structure, not a worse one.**
   B's ladder is arithmetically clean only because it silently fixes the rate at 4% —
   real dividend yield is not a number the investor chooses. **An SWP amount *is*
   chosen.** So the ₹ cut can say the rate out loud on every rung honestly, which is what
   `withdrawal_rate_on_screen` demands and what B fails to do. The ban is a constraint
   that makes us more accurate, not less.
3. **B's reinvest beat gets easier, not harder.** *"You click the button that says
   reinvest"* is a manual act for a dividend investor; in SIP language it is the
   automated monthly debit — a stronger, more familiar image for an Indian audience, and
   a beat that already carries the `assets/lottie/calendar-20th-circled` idea.
4. **The one beat that must be rebuilt is the yield trap — and its ₹ analogue is
   structurally superior.** Both twins warn against a **12% yield**. In an SWP frame the
   identical trap is a **too-high withdrawal rate**: pulling 10–12% a year out of a
   corpus eats the principal and the payout dies. Same beat, same position (~5:00), same
   "if it looks too good it is a warning" line — and it *forces* the withdrawal rate on
   screen, converting a compliance constraint into the video's most dramatic moment.
5. **A does not transfer.** Roughly 8–23% + 58–66% of its runtime is Singapore tax code
   and SGX instruments. India has candidate analogues (the tax treatment of equity MF
   gains versus interest income; the fact that an SWP redemption is partly return of
   capital) but **naming one is fin-facts' job, not this note's** — I will not assert an
   Indian tax claim from a Singapore transcript. What A contributes is: the shape
   (withhold → structural advantage → cost-of-living targets → stated rate → divide →
   path → warning → simplify → recap), and its assumption sentence.
6. **Packaging splits from script.** `run.json` gives the ₹ search volumes: *financial
   freedom* 92,825/mo and *passive income* 46,094/mo versus *dividends* 2,531. Both twins
   show the resolution: **abstraction in the title, bills in the script.** B never says
   "passive income" once and still ranks on the concept. Title/thumbnail carry the
   abstraction; every VO line prices a named monthly bill.
7. **The en/$ cut can run much closer to B verbatim** — it is already a US video, already
   in our target vocabulary, and *"dividend"* is legal there. The `market_rewrite_not_translation`
   constraint means the two cuts share the ladder and share nothing else.
