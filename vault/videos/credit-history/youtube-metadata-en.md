## Description

```
There's a file on you that you've never read, and it decides whether you get a loan and what interest you pay. The lender reads your report before it reads you. It's called your credit report, and its summary is one three-digit number — your FICO score, from 300 to 850. 670 and up is generally called good.

In this video: what that report actually is, what builds it, what destroys it, and why it matters years before you ever need a loan.

What builds it: payment history — did you pay on time — is 35% of your FICO score, and how much of your available credit you're using is another 30%. That's 65% of the whole thing, decided by two habits. Other factors count too; these two are the biggest.

What destroys it, and the part people get wrong: under federal law, most negative information can stay on your credit report for seven years. A bankruptcy, ten. And the clock does not start when you finally pay it off — it starts at the original missed payment. One bad month, seven years of consequences.

What it costs: same $25,000 used car, same 72-month loan, two different scores. Top credit tier runs around 6%. Subprime, around 19% — roughly three times the interest on the identical car. That's about $172 more every month, and around $12,400 more in interest over the life of the loan. Same car. Different number.

Two moves today. One: put every card and every loan on auto-pay, or set a calendar alert for every single due date — remembering shouldn't be your job. Two: pull up your credit report and actually read it; if there's a late mark or an account that isn't yours, dispute it. A score is built over months and years, which is exactly why you start long before you need the loan.
```
